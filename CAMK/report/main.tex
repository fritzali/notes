		\begin{abstract}
			In this work, we present various two dimensional radiation hydrodynamic simulations of a thin viscous accretion disk around a nonrotating
			supermassive black hole with a mass of \qty{1e8}{\solarmass} in the PLUTO code. Starting from an existing star disk setup with
			viscosity and resistivity, we replace the central star with an absorbing inner boundary, use a Paczyński–Wiita instead of the original
			Newtonian potential, and add the nonrelativistic two moment radiation module with Kramers, Thomson and an effective Compton opacity. Once
			radiation is enabled, the advected passive tracer that restricted viscosity, resistivity and opacities to the disk no longer follows
			the disk material. We therefore develop a replacement called \texttt{DiskFraction} which classifies each cell from its density relative
			to adaptive radial reference profiles and based on its rotation. We validate this classifier against the tracer in short hydrodynamic,
			magnetohydrodynamic and resistive runs without radiation, and then use it in two long radiation runs at different resolutions.
			Both reach statistically steady states with accretion rates of about \num{0.07} to \num{0.09} times the Eddington rate and
			a radiative efficiency close to \num{0.048} in both cases. They carry quasiperiodic density waves that travel outward with
			a period of about \num{350} gravitational times, and show a weak inflow stream at intermediate altitudes. We discuss which
			of these features may be artifacts of the fixed sound speed in the viscosity prescription, of the boundaries and of the physics
			still missing from the setup.

	\section{Introduction}

	Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) are thought to be powered by accretion onto Super Massive Black Holes (SMBH) and make up some of
	the brightest objects in the universe. In the standard picture, the surrounding gas forms a geometrically thin disk in which
	an effective viscosity transports angular momentum outward and converts gravitational energy into heat that is radiated away
	locally \cite{shak1973}. For accretion rates around one tenth of the Eddington rate, the inner regions of such systems are
	dominated by radiation pressure, and in this regime the standard disk with viscous stress proportional to the pressure is
	thermally and viscously unstable \cite{ligh1974, shak1976}. Whether real disks follow this prediction, and what happens in
	these cases, is a question that requires following the coupled evolution of gas and radiation in time, which is what numerical
	simulations enable us to do.

	With this work, we build such a setup in the PLUTO code \cite{mign2007}. We start from an existing setup for the interaction of
	a magnetized star with its accretion disk \cite{ceme2019} and adapt it to a black hole before enabling radiation as detailed in
	\Cref{sec:setup}. Along the way, we find that the passive tracer used to separate disk and corona, and with it the physics restricted
	to the disk, stops working once radiation is enabled. Most of our effort therefore went into building a replacement for this tracer,
	the \texttt{DiskFraction} classifier, which we describe and validate in \Cref{sec:classifier}. Using this implementation, we then run
	the radiation setup at two resolutions until both reach a quasi steady state, and present first results in \Cref{sec:results}. The
	most important limitations of this setup are discussed in \Cref{sec:discussion}. Auxiliary equations, the analysis methods, supplementary
	figures as well as a link to all source files are included in the \hyperref[sec:appendix]{Appendix}.

	\section{Numerical Setup}
	\label{sec:setup}

	All simulations use version 4.4 of PLUTO \cite{mign2007} on a two dimensional grid in spherical coordinates, with radius $r$ and
	polar angle $\theta$ from the origin, as well as axisymmetry and equatorial symmetry, meaning that only one hemisphere is simulated.
	Additionally, we also use the cylindrical radius $R = r \sin\theta$ and height $z = r \cos\theta$ above the midplane throughout this text.

	\subsection{Base Model}

	The starting point is a stellar setup from a numerical atlas of such systems \cite{ceme2019} which solves the equations of viscous
	and resistive magnetohydrodynamics for a thin disk around a rotating star with a magnetic field. Its initialization follows an
	analytic thin viscous disk \cite{kluz2000} with aspect ratio $\epsilon = H/R$ and is embedded inside a corona in
	hydrostatic equilibrium. Viscosity and resistivity follow an $\alpha$ prescription and are restricted to the disk by a passive
	tracer, which is advected with the flow and initially equals one in the disk and zero in the corona. To ensure the disk stays thin
	without radiative cooling, the setup also includes an artificial cooling term, which S. Kundu and M. Čemeljić use in their setup.
	It removes a multiple of the local viscous and Ohmic heating from the gas, again weighted by the tracer, wherever this
	removal stays below ten percent of the thermal energy of the cell. Its exact form is given in \Cref{app:equations}.

	\subsection{Black Hole Adaptation}

	Our initial tests ran on the stellar setups of the atlas. For a black hole, we replace the stellar surface at the inner radial
	boundary with an absorbing diode condition, which copies the state of the first active cell but lets gas and radiation only
	flow inward. The gravity of the central star is replaced with the pseudo Newtonian potential \cite{pacz1980}
	\begin{equation}
		\Phi = -\frac{G M}{r - r_s}
	\end{equation}
	where the term $r_s = 2 G M / c^2$ gives the Schwarzschild radius. Within Newtonian dynamics, this potential reproduces the
	innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO) of a nonrotating black hole at six gravitational radii, and with it $\eta = 1/16$ for the
	efficiency of a disk that ends there. Lengths are measured in gravitational radii $r_g = G M / c^2$ and times in gravitational
	times $t_g = r_g / c$ where velocities have units of $c$ accordingly. Without radiation, the equations solved here contain no
	other scale, so all results of the non radiative runs should hold for any black hole mass.

	The domain extends from $r = 2.{\kern-0.5pt}1$ to $r = 30$ in code units on a logarithmic radial grid and from the pole to the
	midplane on a uniform polar grid. The initial disk has $\epsilon = 0.{\kern-0.5pt}1$ and an inner edge at $R = 6$ in these units, while
	the corona has the density profile $\rho_c \propto r^{-3/2}$ with a normalization of \num{0.01} in code units. At the
	outer boundary, the analytic disk is reinjected in a narrow wedge around the midplane, while inflow of coronal gas is
	blocked at higher latitudes. All tests of the tracer and the classifier in \Cref{sec:classifier} were performed in this
	black hole setup, that is for a nonrotating black hole under Newtonian dynamics with the pseudo potential, with and without
	magnetic fields and resistivity.

	\subsection{Radiation \& Physical Units}

	For radiation, we use the nonrelativistic version of the two moment radiation module of PLUTO with the full speed of light
	\cite{melo2019, melo2021}. Radiation introduces physical scales through the opacities as well as the radiation constant, so
	the units have to be fixed. We choose a black hole mass of \qty{1e8}{\solarmass} for all radiation runs, which gives us units of
	$r_g \approx \qty{1.48e13}{\centi\meter}$ and $t_g \approx \qty{493}{\second}$ as the normalization. The density unit then is chosen
	to be $\rho_0 = \qty{5.8e-10}{\gram\per\centi\meter\cubed}$ together with the viscosity parameter $\alpha = 0.5$ set in such a way that
	viscous heating and radiative cooling of the initial disk are roughly balanced, which corresponds to accretion at around a tenth of
	the Eddington rate. A twenty times higher density unit with $\alpha = 1$ gave a heating excess equivalent to strongly super Eddington
	accretion during a previous run, and the disk was disrupted within a few hundred gravitational times. In the radiation runs, the
	artificial cooling is removed, since heat is now carried away by the radiation.

	Regarding opacities, we assume a standard hydrogen mass fraction $X = 0.7$ with a metallicity $Z = 0.02$ and include Kramers
	free free and bound free absorption \cite{kram1923} as well as a term $\kappa_\mathrm{es} = 0.2 \left(1 + X\right)$ for Thomson scattering
	in \unit{\centi\meter\squared\per\gram} units. PLUTO treats scattering as fully coherent, so in this form electron scattering never
	exchanges energy between gas and radiation. Inside AGN disks, however, it is Compton scattering that keeps the gas close to the
	radiation temperature, and without it viscous heating drove the gas in the inner disk to between \num{1e10} and
	\qty{1e11}{\kelvin} in our tests, until overpressured bubbles broke the disk apart. We therefore add an effective absorption
	opacity
	\begin{equation}
		\kappa_C = \kappa_\mathrm{es} \frac{4 k_B T_r^4}{m_e c^2 \left(T_g + T_r\right) \left(T_g^2 + T_r^2\right)}
		\label{eq:compton}
	\end{equation}
	which has gas temperature $T_g$ and radiation temperature $T_r$ as inputs. Given this opacity, including the absorption term
	$\rho \kappa_C c \left(a_R T_g^4 - E_r\right)$ reproduces exactly the nonrelativistic Compton exchange rate
	\begin{equation}
		\Lambda_C = 4 \rho \kappa_\mathrm{es} c E_r \frac{k_B \left(T_g - T_r\right)}{m_e c^2}
	\end{equation}
	since the factor $T_g - T_r$ cancels analytically against $T_g^4 - T_r^4$ in the absorption form. This rate is the usual
	approximation for Compton cooling and heating in radiation hydrodynamics, and terms of this form are included for example in
	radiation magnetohydrodynamic simulations of accretion disks \cite{jian2019}. The Compton part is moved from scattering to absorption, so
	the total extinction stays that of Thomson scattering and only the energy exchange changes. Several safeguards are added to complete the
	opacity model, these are listed in \Cref{app:equations}. They hold the Compton rate fixed above gas temperatures of
	\qty{1e9}{\kelvin} and switch energy exchange off in the corona and in gas that moves faster than about nine tenths of
	the speed of light. A floor of \qty{1e4}{\kelvin} for the gas temperature prevents surface layers from cooling far below the
	range in which the opacity model is valid.

	\section{Disk Classifier}
	\label{sec:classifier}

	In the base model, the passive tracer decides where viscosity, resistivity, artificial cooling and later the opacities act.
	Once radiation is enabled, this tracer stops following the disk material in our runs. It stays at its initial distribution
	while the disk itself evolves, as discussed further below. We did not isolate the cause of this in the code. Instead, we
	replace the tracer with a classifier that derives the disk membership of each cell from its current state alone.

	\subsection{Construction}

	\texttt{DiskFraction} assigns each cell a value $f$ between zero for corona and one for disk from two criteria. The first compares the
	density $\rho$ of the cell with a corona reference density $\rho_c(r)$ and a disk reference density $\rho_d(r)$ at its radius,
	which are stored in \num{90} logarithmic radial bins and interpolated linearly in logarithmic radius and density. With the
	relative position of the cell between these references
	\begin{equation}
		s = \frac{\log \rho - \log \rho_c}{\max\left(\log \rho_d - \log \rho_c\kern+1pt, 0.5\right)}
	\end{equation}
	and the logistic function $\sigma(x) = 1 / \left(1 + e^{-x}\right)$ the classifier reads
	\begin{equation}
		f = \sigma\left(\frac{s - 1{\kern-0.5pt}/6}{0.3}\right) \sigma\left(\frac{\lvert v_\phi \rvert \sqrt{R} - 0.5}{0.{\kern-0.5pt}1}\right)
		\label{eq:diskfrac}
	\end{equation}
	where the second factor compares the rotation velocity $v_\phi$ with the Newtonian Keplerian velocity $R^{-1/2}$ in code units,
	so that only dense and rotating gas counts as disk. The midpoint of the density criterion lies one sixth of the way from the
	corona to the disk reference in logarithmic density. At the disk reference density itself, the first factor is only about
	\num{0.94} and $f$ therefore stays slightly below one inside the disk. At the start of a run, before any profile has been updated, the
	classifier $f$ equals the initial tracer exactly, since both are evaluated from the same condition that sets up the disk itself.

	By default, the references are built from the classification itself. After every time step, the corona reference of a bin
	becomes the mean density weighted with $1\kern-1pt - \kern+0.5ptf$ and the disk reference the mean density weighted with
	$\kern+1pt f \kern-0.5pt$ over all cells in the bin. A disk estimate only counts if its weighted volume exceeds one thousandth
	of the bin volume, and bins that never had one copy the nearest valid bin. Both profiles are smoothed in time by an exponential
	moving average, which gives each new estimate the weight
	\begin{equation}
		w = 1\kern-0.5pt - \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta t}{\tau}\right)
		\label{eq:ema}
	\end{equation}
	with the time $\Delta t$ between two updates and the time constant $\tau = 6 \, t_g$ as parameters. The state of the
	classifier is written with every output, so that restarts continue with exactly the same classification.

	\subsection{Versions \& Guards}

	A first version of the classifier gave each update the fixed weight \num{0.05} instead. Updated after every step, the
	profiles then had a memory of about twenty steps, which depends on resolution and corresponds to approximately $0.{\kern-0.5pt}12$ and
	$0.{\kern-0.5pt}17$ gravitational times for the two configurations described below. In a run with this version at the higher
	resolution, the density criterion inverted after the disk had been disrupted. In bins at radii between about \num{2.4} and \num{3.4} the
	disk reference density remained at about a factor of ten below the corona reference until the end of the run. Since the references kept
	getting built from the classification, this inversion sustained itself, due to dense gas classified as corona raising the corona reference
	and thin gas classified as disk lowering the mean disk value. The rotation criterion was not able to compensate, because any gas plunging
	inside the ISCO keeps its specific angular momentum of about \num{3.7} and therefore rotates two to three times faster than
	the Newtonian Keplerian velocity there. The result was an annular region close to the black hole classified as disk up to about \ang{45}
	above the midplane, while the dense plunging inner stream lost its viscosity and the coupling to radiation.

	Prompted by this, the current version adds multiple guards to the classifier. The logarithmic span between references used must be at least
	\qty{0.5}{\dex} as the denominator of $s$ already includes above, so an inverted span can never flip the
	density criterion. After every update, bins with a smaller span copy both references from the nearest bin with a sufficient
	span, searching outward first, or have their disk reference raised to the minimum span if no such bins exists anywhere. Finally,
	the smoothing uses the time constant of \Cref{eq:ema} instead of a fixed weight, so that the memory of the profiles is the same
	at every resolution and update cadence. Slight artifacts at the inner boundary still appear in this version, but the guards
	let the classifier repair itself instead of locking an error in place.

	As a further option, the references can also be built without reference to the classification. The disk profile then takes the
	maximum density of each bin and the corona reference the geometric mean density of the cells above $z / R = 0.5$ in that bin.
	This mode cannot feed back on itself, but it has not been tested to the same degree as the default and is not used for the results
	presented here.

	\subsection{Validation}

	In testing the classifier, we ran the black hole setup without radiation as hydrodynamic, magnetohydrodynamic and resistive
	magnetohydrodynamic simulations with \num{90} by \num{45} cells up to $t = 1000$ in gravitational times. Each case was run
	twice. In the tracer driven run, the tracer $f_\mathrm{tr}$ decides where viscosity, resistivity and the artificial cooling
	act. In the classifier driven run, \texttt{DiskFraction} takes this role, while the tracer is still advected passively and
	can be compared. \Cref{fig:valid_hd} shows both for the hydrodynamic case. The two runs start identically and still look alike
	at $t = 250$ but from about $t = 500$ onward, the surface layer and the corona of the classifier driven run are somewhat thinner.
	Within the classifier driven run, tracer and classifier mark the same disk body, while the tracer extends further into the lifted
	surface layer. This gas has left the disk in density and rotation, so the classifier counts it as corona, while the tracer
	only remembers where it came from.

	\textfig{valid_hd_text.png}{Hydrodynamic black hole setup without radiation. Density of the run driven by the passive tracer on
	top with that driven by \texttt{DiskFraction} below, and the passive tracer $f_\mathrm{tr}$ versus \texttt{DiskFraction}
	$f_\mathrm{df}$ within the latter run at the bottom.}{fig:valid_hd}

	\Cref{fig:valid_agreement} quantifies this via the agreement metric $1 \kern-0.5pt - \langle\lvert\Delta f\rvert\rangle$ with
	$\Delta f = f_\mathrm{df} - f_\mathrm{tr}$ averaged over the domain. Weighted by mass, the agreement stays around
	\qty{95}{\percent} in all cases, and most of the remaining difference is due to $f$ remaining slightly below one
	inside the disk region. Weighted by volume, the agreement dips to between about \qty{84}{\percent} and \qty{87}{\percent} around
	$t = 450$ while the surface layer is most disturbed, and in the hydrodynamic case falls to about \qty{78}{\percent} at the
	end of the run. The maps of $\Delta f$ in \Cref{fig:valid_diff} show negative differences almost everywhere, most concentrated
	near the surface layer.

	\colfig{valid_agreement_col.png}{Match between $f_\mathrm{df}$ and $f_\mathrm{tr}$ in radiationless classifier driven runs,
	mass \textit{(solid)} and volume \textit{(dashed)} weighted.}{fig:valid_agreement}

	With radiation, the picture changes completely. In the first radiation run, which still lacks any type of Compton coupling and
	in which \texttt{DiskFraction} already determines the physics, overpressured bubbles break up the disk, and the classifier marks
	the lifted, dense and rotating gas as disk material up to large heights. The passive tracer keeps its initial wedge throughout
	the entire run and no longer follows the gas at all, matching the previously described error mode.

	\section{Results}
	\label{sec:results}

	We now turn to the radiation runs with Compton coupling and the classifier included. The LR run uses \num{90} by \num{45} cells and
	reaches $t = 5 \times 10^4$ in code units, with outputs every \num{50} gravitational times. The HR run uses \num{120}
	by \num{90} cells and reaches $t = 4 \times 10^4$ with the same output cadence, which increases to \num{5} gravitational
	times from $t = 3 \times 10^4$ onward to enable a more accurate wave analysis. Apart from the resolution, the two runs differ
	in the version of the classifier, where LR still uses the first version described above, while HR uses the current version with
	all guards. Differences between the runs can therefore not be attributed to resolution alone. For the black hole mass of our setup,
	the run time of HR corresponds to about \num{230} days. The analysis methods are summarized in \Cref{app:methods}.

	\subsection{Evolution \& Relaxation}

	In the first few hundred gravitational times, the disk adjusts to the radiation. It starts to inflate, and a dense layer forms at its
	surface, which then breaks up into sheets that are lifted to several gravitational radii above the midplane.

	After this, the two runs evolve differently, as \Cref{fig:relax_mass} shows. In the HR case, the disk is blown apart. Its mass falls
	from \num{132} to about \num{2} in units of $\rho_0 r_g^3$ by $t \approx 2000$ gravitational times, and the disk stays
	fragmented until about $t = 10500$ before it is rebuilt by about $t = 15000$ and settles at a mass of about \num{18} in the
	same units. For LR the disk mass instead declines smoothly from \num{143} to about \num{22} by $t \approx 5000$ without any
	breakup. We think this is most probably due to the stronger numerical viscosity, diffusivity and damping at lower resolution,
	which can smooth out the perturbations that grow into the HR breakup, although the different classifier versions may also
	contribute. In both runs, the corona loses most of its mass early on and is not replenished, since the outer boundary blocks
	coronal inflow.

	\textfig{early_rho_text.png}{Density of HR in the early evolution. The disk inflates and forms a dense surface layer that
	breaks up.}{fig:early}

	To decide when a run has reached a steady state, we compare running means of the disk mass, the radiation energy, the
	accretion rate at the inner boundary and the luminosity at the outer boundary with their mean over the last fifth of the run.
	The steady state begins when all four stay within two standard errors of the reference, which happens at $t = 23725$ for HR
	and $t = 9225$ for LR in gravitational times. All averages in the following are taken over these windows, and within them
	the drift of the averages is consistent with zero.

	\vspace{-0.5ex}
	\colfig{relax_mass_col.png}{Mass split by \texttt{DiskFraction} for both radiation runs. Time is linear until
	$t = 1.1 \times 10^4$ and logarithmic after.\vspace{-1.75ex}}{fig:relax_mass}

	\subsection{Steady State}

	\Cref{fig:domain} depicts the initial density setup of HR next to its average over the last \num{10000}
	gravitational times. In the steady state, the disk is thinner than initially. At $R = 10$ its aspect ratio is about \num{0.057}
	in HR and \num{0.042} in LR compared with $0.{\kern-0.5pt}1$ at the start, which means that LR resolves the disk with only about one cell
	per scale height against about three in the HR run. The corona has drained down to the density floor, and the unit optical depth
	surface lies inside the disk. The accretion rate in \Cref{fig:mdot} is flat in radius out to about $r = 17$
	in both runs, so that the inner disk is in inflow equilibrium. Further out, the disk has not yet relaxed, which matches the
	estimate that viscous inflow reaches radii of \num{26} and \num{30} within the HR and LR run times. Beyond $r = 17$ we get a
	net accretion rate that is the difference of a mean inflow of roughly \num{0.2} Eddington rates and an outflow around $0.{\kern-0.5pt}13$
	in the HR case, so the gas there circulates strongly. The corona carries twelve percent of the accretion at the inner boundary.

	\colfig{mdot_col.png}{Time averaged accretion rate, positive for inflow, in total and split into disk versus corona, as well as
	inflows and outflows. Shaded bands show HR standard errors.}{fig:mdot}

	At the inner boundary, in Eddington normalized units, the mean accretion rate is \num{0.073} in HR and \num{0.091} in LR with the
	Eddington rate defined via the efficiency $\eta = 1/16$ from above. In solar masses, this corresponds to about \num{0.30} and
	\qty{0.38}{\solarmass\per\year} respectively. The luminosity leaving the outer boundary is \num{0.056} and \num{0.070} times
	the Eddington luminosity, or about \qty{8.3e44}{\erg\per\second} and $1{\kern-0.5pt}.0\times 10^{45}\kern+0.5pt\unit{\erg\per\second}$
	between the runs. The ratio of both gives a radiative efficiency of about \num{0.048} in both runs, somewhat below the value for a disk
	that radiates all its binding energy. The vertical flux at the photosphere matches the vertically integrated viscous heating from about
	$r = 8$ outward, but falls short of it further in, as \Cref{fig:balance} shows. Part of the heat released close to the
	black hole is therefore likely advected inward instead of being radiated, which is consistent with the lower efficiency.

	For a steady thin disk with a stress free inner edge at six gravitational radii, the surface density follows
	\begin{equation}
		\Sigma_\mathrm{ss} = \frac{\dot M}{3\pi\nu} \left(1 - \sqrt{6 / R}\kern+1pt\right)
		\label{eq:sigma}
	\end{equation}
	with the kinematic viscosity $\nu$ of our prescription defined in \Cref{app:equations}. The measured surface density in
	\Cref{fig:sigma} lies below this profile at almost all radii except for a ring like maximum around $R = 17$ once the inflow
	equilibrium ends.

	\textfigtight{domain_rho_fit_text.png}{Density of HR run initially \textit{(left)} and averaged \textit{(right)} over the last
	\num{10000} gravitational times.}{fig:domain}

	\colfigtight{sigma_col.png}{Time averaged surface density with steady state profile for given accretion rate.
	Shaded bands show errors.\vspace{-0.75em}}{fig:sigma}

	A surface density below the steady state disk profile indicates that the transport of angular momentum is more efficient than the
	prescribed viscosity alone would allow. Inverting \Cref{eq:sigma} for an effective viscosity parameter gives values of about
	two to three times the prescribed $\alpha$ in both runs as shown in \Cref{fig:alpha} except for a narrow dip to about the
	prescribed value at the ring. The additional transport may come from resolved flows such as the waves below, or from
	numerical effects, which we cannot separate here.\vspace{0.25em}

	\colfigtight{alpha_col.png}{Effective viscosity parameter from the measured accretion rate and surface density, relative to the imposed value.
	Dotted lines mark inflow equilibrium radii per run.\vspace{-0.5em}}{fig:alpha}

	The thermal balance of the disk is dominated by Compton exchange. \Cref{fig:heating_times} compares the time over which viscous
	heating would double the thermal energy of the gas at the midplane with the corresponding cooling times by Compton exchange and
	by Kramers absorption. Between $R = 8$ and $R = 15$ the heating time sits at about \num{0.2} gravitational times, the Compton
	cooling time slightly shorter, and the Kramers cooling time two to three orders of magnitude longer.

	\colfigtight{heating_times_col.png}{Midplane times for viscous heating, Compton and Kramers cooling, with the inverse
	orbital frequency for comparison. Lines are medians over the steady state window and bands show the standard error
	range for the HR run.}{fig:heating_times}

	Heating and cooling therefore balance closely, with a ratio of about \num{0.8} to \num{0.9} as can be seen in \Cref{fig:heating_ratio}.
	This balance is reached far from local thermodynamic equilibrium, however. The median gas temperature at the midplane falls
	from about \qty{1.7e8}{\kelvin} at $R = 6$ to roughly \qty{1e7}{\kelvin} at $R = 16$ while the radiation temperature stays at
	about \qty{8e4}{\kelvin} so that the gas is a hundred to almost two thousand times hotter than the radiation. Since the
	Compton rate grows with the difference of both temperatures, the gas has to heat up to these values before it can radiate the
	viscous heat, as Kramers absorption is far too weak in the present conditions.

	\colfig{heating_ratio_col.png}{Ratio of viscous heating to radiative cooling at the midplane, with the standard error range as bands.}{fig:heating_ratio}

	\subsection{Density Waves}

	Both runs in their steady states exhibit quasiperiodic density waves that travel outward. \Cref{fig:delta} shows relative surface
	density perturbations, where they appear as ridges that start near the ISCO and propagate outward to radii of $R = 17$ and beyond.
	Their root mean square amplitude, relative to the mean surface density, grows from about \num{0.25} near the inner
	boundary to about \num{0.8} around $R = 9$ and beyond $R = 20$ with a dip near $R = 15$ right inside the ring anomaly, making the waves
	strongly nonlinear. Power spectra of these perturbations peak at a frequency of roughly \num{2.9e-3} per code time in both
	runs, equal to a period of \num{349} gravitational times or about two days for our SMBH mass.

	The phase of the perturbation at this frequency grows steadily with radius as shown in \Cref{fig:phase}. Velocity and radiation energy
	perturbations at the midplane are roughly in phase with the density perturbation between radii of \num{6} and \num{12} as for an
	outward traveling compression, which we depict in \Cref{fig:rms_cross} in the \hyperref[sec:appendix]{Appendix}.

	\colfig{spacetime_delta_dense_col.png}{Relative surface density perturbation of HR over the last \num{3000} gravitational times
		at the dense output cadence. The dotted line marks the innermost stable orbit.}{fig:delta}

	The phase speed in \Cref{fig:speed} is about \num{0.019} times the speed of light between radii of about \num{4} and \num{17} in
	both runs. This is about \num{2.2} times the adiabatic sound speed including radiation pressure and \num{6.5} times the sound speed
	of the gas in HR alone. The waves are therefore not plain sound waves, and we have not identified their
	nature. That the periods are the same in both runs, despite their different resolutions and classifier versions, suggests that
	it is set by the setup rather than by numerics. It is close to the thermal time $1 / (\alpha \Omega)$ of about \num{330}
	gravitational times at the outer boundary, which may hint at an origin there, possibly together with the fixed sound speed
	in the viscosity, but this remains to be tested. Beyond the ring at $R = 17$ the accretion rate and the enclosed mass
	oscillate with about \num{670} gravitational times instead, roughly twice the wave period.

	\subsection{Inflow Stream}

	Most of the accretion happens close to the midplane. In the steady state of the HR run, roughly half of the net inflow passes within
	about one to three degrees of the midplane and nine tenths within about five to eight degrees at most radii, with $r = {\kern-0.5pt}10$
	as an exception in HR at about twenty degrees, while the disk surface sits between five and nine degrees, so that about \qty{90}{\percent}
	of the inflow passes through the disk. Above the disk, the angular distribution of the inflow in \Cref{fig:angular} shows a second,
	much weaker maximum between about \num{20} and \num{50} degrees of elevation, at around a thousandth of the midplane value per degree.
	It belongs to a faint stream falling toward the central black hole at intermediate altitudes.
	This feature is also visible as a diagonal shape in the averaged density of \Cref{fig:domain} and may well be an artifact of the setup.
	The corona begins from Newtonian hydrostatic equilibrium but evolves in the deeper modified potential, and the outer boundary changes its
	treatment of flows into the domain at intermediate latitudes. We have not tested either explanation.

	\section{Discussion and Outlook}
	\label{sec:discussion}

	Several features of the setup limit how far the results can be interpreted physically. Perhaps the most important one concerns the
	viscosity. Its sound speed is not derived from the evolving gas but instead from the initial disk profile at the same position, meaning it
	does not depend on pressure and is fixed in time. Stresses therefore cannot grow when the material heats up, which removes exactly
	the feedback responsible for driving the thermal and viscous instability of radiation pressure dominated disks \cite{ligh1974, shak1976}. The stability
	of our steady states is thus no test of this instability.

	The boundaries also affect the results. The disk reinjected at the outer boundary and the blocked coronal inflow set the
	supply of gas, the corona drains early and is not replenished, and the inflow stream discussed above as well as the wave period may
	originate there. The mass budget of the steady state does not close either. The change of enclosed mass and the integrated
	fluxes through the boundaries differ by about three percent between the radii \num{2.5} and \num{25} and by about \num{18}
	percent for the full domain, far beyond the sampling error and with a sign that the density floors, which can only add mass,
	cannot explain. This points to the boundary treatment or to the cell centered fluxes used in our diagnostic, as discussed in
	\Cref{app:methods}.

	Further limitations are due to suspicious or missing physics. The gas temperatures are probably too high for our setup to be fully trusted.
	Inside the disk, the matter stays two to three orders of magnitude hotter than the radiation, because only the Compton exchange can carry
	its heat away, and the corona reaches up to about ${\kern-0.5pt}10^{1{\kern-1pt}1}\unit{\kelvin}$ in our runs, where $k_B T$ is about
	\num{46} times the electron rest energy. The nonrelativistic Compton rate is far outside its validity there, which is why we hold it
	fixed above \qty{1e9}{\kelvin} in the opacity model. The setup also includes neither relativistic corrections to the Compton scattering
	nor pair production, which would both matter at these temperatures. With Newtonian dynamics in the pseudo potential, the plunging
	gas inside about four gravitational radii moves superluminally, with radial velocities of up to about two times and rotation
	velocities of ${\kern-0.5pt}1{\kern-0.5pt}.4$ times the speed of light at $r = 2.5$ in gravitational radii. The radiation
	there is decoupled from the gas to keep the nonrelativistic radiation module stable, but the dynamics of this region are
	unphysical. Finally, the simulations are axisymmetric and hydrodynamic, so turbulence driven by the magnetorotational
	instability is replaced by the $\alpha$ prescription, and LR barely resolves the disk vertically with only about one cell per scale
	height.

	Natural next steps would be a viscosity which derives its sound speed from the evolving total pressure, and thereby allows the disk to
	become thermally unstable, a larger coarse domain that moves the outer boundary away from the region of interest, and an initial disk
	that is better suited to radiation pressure dominated accretion, such as a disk built from the standard thin disk solution
	\cite{shak1973}.

	\section{Conclusions}

	We adapted a viscous and resistive star disk setup in PLUTO to a nonrotating supermassive black hole and added radiation with
	effective Compton coupling. Since the passive tracer that restricts the disk physics fails once radiation is enabled, we developed the
	\texttt{DiskFraction} classifier, which agrees with the tracer to about \qty{95}{\percent} percent by mass in runs without radiation, follows the
	disk material with radiation, and in its current version repairs its own errors. With it, two radiation runs at different
	resolutions reach steady states at roughly a tenth of the Eddington rate, in which Compton exchange balances the viscous heating
	closely, with a gas temperature far above the radiation temperature and the effective viscosity far exceeding the prescribed one by a factor
	of two to three, as well as strongly nonlinear density waves with a period of about \num{350} gravitational times traveling outward.
	Whether these features and the weak inflow stream at intermediate altitudes are physical or artifacts of the fixed sound speed,
	the boundaries and the missing high temperature physics has to be tested with the improvements outlined above.

	\subsection{Model Equations}
	\label{app:equations}

	All quantities in this section are in code units unless stated otherwise. The corona starts with the density
	$\rho = \rho_c r^{-3/2}$ and pressure $p = 0.4 \rho_c r^{-5/2}$ where $\rho_c = 0.01$ is assumed. With the function
	\begin{equation}
		C = \frac{2}{5 \epsilon^2} \left(\frac{1}{r} - \frac{1 - 5 \epsilon^2 / 2}{R}\right)
	\end{equation}
	the disk has the pressure $p = \epsilon^2 C^{5/2}$ and density $\rho = C^{3/2}$ wherever its pressure exceeds that of the
	corona and $R > 6$ holds, and its velocities follow the same analytic solution \cite{kluz2000}. In the radiation runs,
	the pressure of the analytic disk is shared between gas and radiation at a common temperature, which makes the initial
	disk radiation pressure dominated.

	The dynamic shear viscosity is
	\begin{equation}
		\eta_\nu = \frac{2}{3} \alpha \rho \epsilon^2 C R^{3/2} f
	\end{equation}
	where $\epsilon^2 C$ is the squared sound speed of the initial disk at the position of the cell, which is fixed in time. At
	the midplane, this produces the kinematic viscosity $\nu = 2 / 3 \,\alpha \epsilon^2 R^{1{\kern-0.5pt}/2}$ that \Cref{eq:sigma} uses.
	In the magnetic runs, the viscosity is also switched off where the ratio of thermal to poloidal magnetic pressure falls below
	\num{0.5} in a cell, and the resistivity follows an alpha prescription of the same form. In the tracer driven runs, the tracer takes
	the place of $f$ here and in the cooling.

	The artificial cooling of S. Kundu and M. Čemeljić removes in each step the energy
	\begin{equation}
		\Delta E = k \, \Delta t \, f \, q
	\end{equation}
	from a cell, where $q$ is the local viscous or Ohmic heating rate with $k = 2$ for viscous and $k = 1$ for Ohmic contributions.
	The removal is only applied where it is less than one tenth of the thermal energy density $p / (\gamma - 1)$ of the cell.
	It is not used in the radiation runs.

	The Kramers opacity in \unit{\cgs} units is
	\begin{equation}
		\kappa_K = \left(K_\mathrm{ff} + K_\mathrm{bf}\right) \rho \, T_g^{-7/2}
	\end{equation}
	with $K_\mathrm{ff} = 3.68 \times 10^{22} \left(1 - Z\right) \left(1 + X\right)$ the free free and
	$K_\mathrm{bf} = 4.34 \times 10^{25} Z \left(1 + X\right) \!/ 10$ the bound free coefficients, where the factor ten in the second is a
	guillotine factor, and the gas temperature in this expression has at least \qty{1e4}{\kelvin} in all cases. Above the
	$T_\mathrm{max} = \qty{1e9}{\kelvin}$ threshold, the Compton opacity of \Cref{eq:compton} is replaced by
	\begin{equation}
		\kappa_C = \kappa_\mathrm{es} \frac{4 k_B T_r^4 \left(T_\mathrm{max} - T_r\right)}{m_e c^2 \left(T_g^4 - T_r^4\right)}
	\end{equation}
	which holds the exchange rate at its $T_g = T_\mathrm{max}$ value and matches \Cref{eq:compton} there. It vanishes completely once the
	radiation temperature itself were to exceed the cap, while the code limits $\kappa_C$ to $\kappa_\mathrm{es}$ in all cases so that the
	scattering opacity stays non negative. Since the rotation criterion never vanishes completely, \texttt{DiskFraction} leaves a value of
	about one thousandth in the corona. Together with the hot coronal gas, this was enough to Compton cool the corona in our tests,
	so the energy exchange uses the gated fraction
	\begin{equation}
		f_d = \max\left(\frac{f - 0.05}{0.95}, \, 0\right)
	\end{equation}
	instead. Near the black hole, the nonrelativistic radiation module becomes unstable for gas moving close to the speed of light,
	so all opacities are multiplied by the factor
	\begin{equation}
		g_v = \left[1 + \exp\left(\frac{\beta - 0.9}{0.03}\right)\right]^{-1}
	\end{equation}
	with the gas speed $\beta = \lvert \mathbf{v} \rvert / c$ as input. The absorption and scattering opacities passed to PLUTO
	are then
	\begin{align}
		\kappa_\mathrm{abs} &= g_v \,f_d \left(\kappa_K + \kappa_C\right) \\
		\kappa_\mathrm{sc} &= g_v \left(\kappa_\mathrm{es} - f_d \kappa_C\right)
	\end{align}
	so that only Thomson scattering acts on all gas. In an earlier version, scattering was multiplying by \texttt{DiskFraction} also.
	Radiation could then only push on gas classified as disk, and gas lifted across the disk surface lost its support and piled up into
	a dense sheet there, which motivated this change, although there was little change during short runs.

	The density floor is set to $\rho = \num{5e-7}$ in code units, applied with a constant sound speed and momentum. At the inner
	boundary, all variables are copied from the first active cell, with radial velocity and radiation flux limited to inflow. At
	the outer boundary, density is extrapolated with a limited logarithmic slope that may not rise outward, the pressure follows
	adiabatically, and radiation leaves freely. Within an angle of $\arctan(1.25 \epsilon)$ from the midplane the analytic disk
	is reinjected, and beyond $\arctan(3 \epsilon)$ any inflowing velocity is set to zero.

	\subsection{Analysis Methods}
	\label{app:methods}

	Time averages use the regular coarse output cadence of \num{50} gravitational times, and their standard errors are estimated from
	the scatter of block averages over \num{1000} gravitational times \cite{flyv1989}, which accounts for the correlation between
	consecutive outputs.

	For the relaxation of a global quantity $X$ we compute running means $\bar X_W$ over windows of $W\kern-1pt = 2500$ gravitational
	times, centered on $t_c$ and compare them with the mean $X_\mathrm{ref}$ over the last fifth of the run through the statistic
	\begin{equation}
		D(t_c) = \frac{\lvert \bar X_W(t_c) - X_\mathrm{ref} \rvert}{\sigma}
	\end{equation}
	where $\sigma$ combines the standard errors of both means. Any quantity counts as relaxed from the first window after which
	$D$ stays below two until the reference period, and the steady state begins when all four quantities in \Cref{sec:results}
	are relaxed. \Cref{fig:relax_distance} shows this statistic for the HR run.

	The radius of inflow equilibrium is the largest radius out to which the running mean of the accretion rate stays within
	twenty percent of its value inside six gravitational radii, and we quote here the median over the steady state window. The viscous
	estimate in \Cref{sec:results} solves $t = R^2 / \nu$ for the radius.

	Power spectra as well as cross spectra of the surface density perturbation $\delta = \Sigma / \langle \Sigma \rangle - 1$ use the
	averaged periodogram method \cite{welc1967} with Hann windows, half overlapping segments of at most \num{2500} gravitational timescales and linear
	detrending. The found peak frequency is refined by parabolic interpolation in logarithmic power. The phase of the cross spectrum
	with reference radius $r = 7$ at the peak frequency is unwrapped in radius, and only those radii with a coherence above its five
	percent significance level as well as a sufficient share of power at the peak are used. The local phase speed follows from a linear
	fit of the phase against radius over a range of fifteen percent in radius as
	$v_p = \omega / (\mathrm{d}\varphi / \mathrm{d}r)$ with the angular frequency $\omega$ of the peak.

	The mass budget compares the change of mass between two radii with the time integral of the accretion rates through them.
	Both are taken from the dense outputs of HR where the accretion rates are evaluated at cell centers. Their difference would
	be the mass added by the density floor, and the error of the time integration is estimated from integrals over even and odd
	outputs separately. \Cref{fig:budget} shows that this difference is negative, which the floor cannot cause.

	The heating and cooling times of \Cref{fig:heating_times} use the viscous heating rate term
	$q^+ = \eta_\nu \left(R \, \partial_R \Omega\right)^2$ at the midplane and the exchange terms of the gated Compton and Kramers
	opacities with the same gates as in the simulation, each relative to the thermal energy density of the gas.
