\section{Spectral Characteristics}
\label{sec:characteristic}
\vspace{-0.5\baselineskip}

The appearance of \xray spectra measured as the emission from galaxy clusters is governed by the physics
at their origin in a high temperature, diffuse plasma. When working with data that have not been
fully cleaned, such as those shown in \Cref{fig:chan_spectra} from the \textit{Chandra}
observatory, we are additionally including instrument noise that has to be disentangled from real
physical effects before the analysis. In our case, peaks that look like emission lines are in fact
fluorescence artifacts from cosmic ray particles hitting the spacecraft materials. A telltale sign
for their contaminating nature is the perfect alignment in frequency where we would expect displacement
caused by the different $z$ values of sources at varying cosmic redshifts. Practically, this means that
the spiking relative count rates at \qty{2.0}{\kilo\electronvolt} and \qty{7.4}{\kilo\electronvolt} as
well as near \qty{9.7}{\kilo\electronvolt} should be subtracted from the continuum. Furthermore, the
flattening or even slight increase of the smooth component beyond about \qty{6.6}{\kilo\electronvolt}
is likewise a result of the gentle power law slope of the cosmic ray spectrum overshooting the signal
\cite{boeh2010, weis2000}.

\subsection{Continuum}

For galaxy clusters, the baseline continuum is virtually fully dominated by ICM thermal bremsstrahlung.
This prediction is supported by \Cref{fig:chan_spectra} and allows the reconstruction of an average
temperature from its slope by assuming a roughly isothermal system. Some observations also hint at
possible excesses, both in the soft and hard \xray regimes. Provided these signals are not some kind of
calibration error, possible causes are the coexistence of multiphase plasma components, a type
of phenomenon that seems especially likely in the case of stratified CC systems, weak emission
from WHIM bremsstrahlung, or upscattering of background photons in shocks such as those in NCC
clusters, all of which could be introducing nonthermal signatures in a single temperature model \cite{boeh2010}.


\subsection{Line Emission}

Superimposed on the continuous bremsstrahlung spectrum are narrow emission lines from highly ionized
ICM elements. The only prominent physical feature in \Cref{fig:chan_spectra} is the Fe~K complex
spread between 6 and \qty{7}{\kilo\electronvolt} depending on redshift. In it, the most important lines
stem from the hydrogen like Fe~XXVI and helium like Fe~XXV ions with \qty{6.97}{\kilo\electronvolt} and
{6\kern+0.5pt.\kern-0.5pt}\qty{70}{\kilo\electronvolt} restframe energies, respectively. Since the potential of \qty{8.83}{\kilo\electronvolt}
required to remove the last electron from the Fe nucleus is extremely high, there are very few electrons in the Maxwell
distribution tail with velocities energetic enough at typical CC central temperatures, whereas massive NCC clusters
routinely exceed the required thermal energy. Due to this fact, the line ratio of both dominant Fe~K lines is an
excellent thermometer since it can be well fitted under CIE assumptions. Analogously, we can use the relative overall
abundances of the high temperature Fe~K to low energy Fe~L line forests to infer ICM thermal properties. 

Another kind of analysis is enabled by taking an accounting of the O and Ne abundances through their H and He like lines
in the coldest regions of cluster cores. Their ratio to the iron peak elements like Fe and Ni traces the occurrence rate
between thermonuclear SN{\kern+1pt}Ia and core collapse SN{\kern+1pt}II supernovae throughout all evolutionary history
of the system. Where the first type produces only negligible traces of O and Ne but massive amounts of Fe and Ni elements,
the second kind does release large quantities of these lighter alpha process elements, which therefore represent a unique fingerprint.
Correlating the relationship between supernova types with redshift, we can reveal the Initial Mass Function (IMF) of stellar populations
throughout cosmic time. Studies that make use of this approach usually identify little to no deviation from the Salpeter IMF
\begin{equation*}
	\xi(M_*) \kern-1pt = dN\kern-1pt/dM_* \propto M_*^{-\alpha}
\end{equation*}
with $\alpha = 2.3$ which holds for the local universe. Lastly, by instead analyzing ratios such as Ni to Fe within the iron peak
elements themselves, it is possible to distinguish between different SN{\kern+1pt}Ia models. From this, slow deflagration models assuming
a subsonic flame can be ruled out, leaving delayed detonations with a supersonic shock burning through the near Chandrasekhar mass limit
white dwarf as the probable main drivers for pollution of the ICM with iron peak elements \cite{boeh2010}.
