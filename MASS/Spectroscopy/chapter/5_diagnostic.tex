As a fundamental theoretical baseline, galaxy clusters are modeled as systems governed purely by gravitation. Due to the
scale invariance of gravity, they should then only differ by predictable mathematical scalings that depend solely on mass or
temperature. In reality, Supermassive Black Hole (SMBH) accretion through AGN feedback and cooling in the cluster core disturb
this idealized view. To understand these physics, we compare the deviations of several key ICM properties from our naive
expectation about their behavior.

Most generally, the procedure of how to extract properties from a measured \xray spectrum involves a computational CIE plasma
model fitting the best estimators for temperature, density, abundances and redshift to the given. Perhaps the most famous ones
are the legacy MEKAL and modern APEC codes, which are both implemented in the XSPEC software. Historical observations could
often not resolve the extended ICM and used simpler isothermal models to extract data. As discussed previously, this is at
best a rough approximation and at worst completely inaccurate. Thanks to advances in current \xray telescopes, we have access
to resolved ICM halo datasets from which we can extract radial distributions, although there are still large uncertainties about
nonthermal support from turbulent eddies or bulk movement indicated by hydrodynamical simulations plaguing the field that bias our
results towards lower total masses. Due to the revolution of microcalorimeter spectrographs on board newer missions, it is however
expected that these unknowns will be solved with higher precision measurements \cite{zhan2026}.

To translate our two dimensional images to radial profiles, we need to correct for the projection from three dimensions along the
line of sight to the detector. The basic algorithm do this decomposes our cluster into multiple annuli around the central core.
Starting from the outermost annulus, we fit our spectrum and since there is little pollution from foreground or background layers,
we assume that it represents a uniform shell around the whole system, taken as radially symmetric. Using this, we compute the
corrections which have to be subtracted from the adjacent inner annulus and fit our plasma model to its now cleaned spectrum. By
iteratively peeling layer by layer from the cluster, we deproject our image onto the radius of the system. For CC clusters this
is enough, but for highly disrupted NCC cases more sophisticated methods are needed \cite{boeh2010}.

From the employed plasma model, we know the best fitting density $n$ and temperature $T$ parameters. With these, we can simply apply
the ideal gas law
\begin{equation*}
	P = nkT
\end{equation*}
to calculate the pressure. Similarly, we get
\begin{equation*}
	K \kern-1pt= kT n^{-2/3}
\end{equation*}
for the astrophysical entropy. This quantity is related to its thermodynamic counterpart as $S \propto \ln(K)$ and tracks parcels
under adiabatic evolution. To prove this statement, we can take the relation $P \propto V^{-5/3} \propto n^{5/3}$ for a blob expanding
without the exchange of heat to its environment and find the proportionality $kT \kern-1pt\propto n^{2/3}$ by plugging in the ideal gas
law, meaning that $K$ indeed remains constant. This makes it an incredibly useful indicator of the dynamical state of a cluster and
allows us to draw conclusions on its recent past. While the purely gravitational baseline assumption produces smoothly falling entropy
profiles towards the core, lower values are typically a result of radiative cooling in a relaxed configuration, whereas a flat or elevated
profile indicates an injection of energy from central engines like SMBH driven feedback and galactic winds from star formation, or merger
events with other galaxy clusters \cite{boeh2010, sara1986, rosa2002}.

We can reproduce qualitatively and even to a certain extent quantitatively the shapes of radial profiles using fairly simple equations.
For a typical NCC cluster, number density is sufficiently well described by a single beta model
\begin{equation*}
	n(r) = n_0 \!\left(1 \kern-0.5pt + r^2 \kern-1pt/ r_c^2 \right)^{-3\kern-1pt\beta\kern-0.5pt/\kern+0.5pt 2}
\end{equation*}
in the entire system. For the CC case, we superimpose the brightness $B \propto n^2$ from an inner cool and dense core with the
extended sparse halo. We use a double beta model
\begin{equation*}
	n(r) = \sqrt{n_1^2 + n_2^2}
\end{equation*}
where $n_1$ and $n_2$ correspond to their respective substructure. When modeling the temperature profile, a broken power law is a decent fit
for NCC systems. To incorporate the decline towards the CC central regions, we include a cooling factor. We then write the combined formula
\begin{equation*}
	T(r) = T_0 \,\frac{x_\text{cool} + T_\text{min} / T_0 \vphantom{^{\int}}}{x_\text{cool} + 1}
	\,\frac{(r / r_t)^{-a}}{\bigl(1 + (r / r_t)^b \bigr)^{c/b}}
\end{equation*}
with $x_\text{cool} = (r / r_\text{cool})^{a_\text{cool}}$ and where the cooling term is only significant in CC clusters that have
$T_\text{min} < T_0$ since for NCC we set $T_\text{min} \approx T_0$ so that it is unity \cite{vikh2006}. As defined before,
pressure $P$ and entropy $K$ can be derived directly from the $n$ and $T$ profiles, or we can introduce their own scaling relations.
First, with an averaged overdensity concentration $C_\ell$ and $x_\ell = r / R_\ell$ we use
\begin{equation*}
	P(r) = P_0 \!\left( \kern-1pt(C_\ell \,x_\ell)^c \bigl( 1 + (C_\ell \,x_\ell)^a \bigr) \kern-1pt \right)^{-(b - c) / a}
\end{equation*}
as a universal pressure profile \cite{arna2010}. Second, we get a simple power law with constant floor
\begin{equation*}
	K(r) = K_0 + K_\ell \, x_\ell^\gamma
\end{equation*}
to describe entropy \cite{cava2009}. This function agrees with both empirical and theoretical models. Selecting parameter values
motivated by realistic ranges and readable graphical appearances, \Cref{fig:theo_profiles} depicts radial profiles for the
introduced quantities. Accounting for their simplicity, they match the observed distributions in \Cref{fig:chan_profiles,fig:xmmn_profiles}
remarkably well, with most of the deviation stemming from the inconsistent normalizations and radial distance ranges.
