	\section{Introduction}

	With ongoing and upcoming surveys providing ever larger amounts of data, the task of object classification is
	becoming increasingly relevant. In this work, we will summarize and compare select methods for characterizing
	narrow emission line galaxies and the extraction of their physical properties. For all steps in the following
	sections, we use data from the latest SDSS data release and match those to spectra obtained by WISE for the
	same objects \cite{wrig2010, koll2026}. Information to reproduce these results are included in the \hyperref[sec:appendix]{appendix}.

	\section{Optical \& Infrared Classification}

	Generally, the broad labeling of galaxies as Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) or Star Formation (SF) dominated types
	relies on reconstructing the excitation and ionization mechanisms from emission lines, and determining whether
	these match the thermal continuum of stellar populations or the strongly nonthermal radiation produced in AGN
	powered by Super Massive Black Holes (SMBH) in their centers. Approaches analyzing the continuum emission provide
	complementary insights, and by probing different wavelengths we can peer through obscuring dust and gas to unveil
	the true underlying physics. Now, we will explore some famous examples.

	\begin{figure}[t]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth, draft=false]{assets/plots/BPT_scatter.pdf}\vspace{-0.5\baselineskip}
		\caption{Standard BPT diagram and class separation curves \cite{kewl2001, kauf2003}.}
		\label{fig:BPT_scatter}

	\end{figure}
	
	\subsection{BPT Diagram}

	By plotting the ratio of emission line fluxes for $\mathrm{\raisebox{0.75pt}{[\raisebox{-0.75pt}{OIII}]}}$
	at \qty{5007}{\angstrom} to $\mathrm{H}\beta$ at \qty{4863}{\angstrom} versus $\mathrm{\raisebox{0.75pt}{[\raisebox{-0.75pt}{NII}]}}$
	at \qty{6583}{\angstrom} to $\mathrm{H}\alpha$ at \qty{6565}{\angstrom} in their work, Baldwin,
	Phillips, and Terlevich draw a diagram that sorts galaxies into a distinctive winged shape \cite{bald1981}.
	Due to lacking the energies to ionize heavier atoms, SF types tend towards the lower left of the image, while
	powerful AGN fall into the upper right tip. By choosing emission lines close to one another, this framework
	automatically cancels out reddening differences as well as calibration errors. Depicted in \Cref{fig:BPT_scatter}
	is our entire dataset as a scatter plot that has been shaded based on the density of nearby points. This convention
	is adopted for all such diagrams in this work for better interpretability of the data. Together with this, standard
	theoretical \cite{kewl2001} and empirical \cite{kauf2003} curves to discriminate between AGN and SF classes
	are shown.

	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth, draft=false]{assets/plots/BPT_class.pdf}\vspace{-0.5\baselineskip}
		\caption{Colored BPT diagram with derived class labels.}
		\label{fig:BPT_class}
	\end{figure}

	We label points between the lines as COMP for composite types and display the classes in \Cref{fig:BPT_class} encoded by color.
	Additionally, we have access to the already processed SDSS labels which we can similarly plot in the BPT diagram. This is done
	in \Cref{fig:BPT_SDSS_class} for the AGN and SF as well as starburst SB subclasses. Comparing to the previous plot gives a
	first visual impression of their cross method agreement, which for the AGN wing seems acceptable, but will be evaluated more
	rigorously in the coming paragraphs.

	\end{figure}

	\subsection{WHAN Diagram}

	Next up is the WHAN diagram, which plots the equivalent width $W_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}$ of the $\mathrm{H}\alpha$ line against
	the ratio of $\mathrm{\raisebox{0.75pt}{[\raisebox{-0.75pt}{NII}]}}$ to $\mathrm{H}\alpha$ fluxes \cite{cidf2011}. This width
	quantifies line strengths in terms of the underlying continuum and is thereby independent of measurement effects. The concept
	behind a classification using the measure is that based on the prominence of lines above the background radiation, we
	can estimate the relative photon budget of the exciting or ionizing radiation field. In true AGN and SF galaxies, this value
	is high, whereas retired galaxies with old stellar populations have less pronounced emission lines. This is a distinction
	which the BPT classifier fails to take into account and can therefore improve our label confidence.

	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth, draft=false]{assets/plots/WHAN_scatter.pdf}
		\caption{Raw data in WHAN diagram with separators.}
		\label{fig:WHAN_scatter}
	\end{figure}
	
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth, draft=false]{assets/plots/WHAN_class.pdf}
		\caption{Colored WHAN diagram with derived labels.}
		\label{fig:WHAN_class}
	\end{figure}

	With the raw scattered data, \Cref{fig:WHAN_scatter} includes discrimination lines. According to these, any galaxy
	with $W_{\mathrm{H}\alpha} < \qty{3.0}{\angstrom}$ is classified as rGAL or retired. Points above this value with
	$\log_{10}(\mathrm{\raisebox{0.75pt}{[\raisebox{-0.75pt}{NII}]}} \mathbin{/} \mathrm{H}\alpha) > -0.4$ are labeled
	AGN or else pSF for pure star formation. Within the active class, we also include the distinction for strong
	sAGN if $W_{\mathrm{H}\alpha} > \qty{6.0}{\angstrom}$ and weak wAGN otherwise. There is further a category defined via
	$W_{\mathrm{H}\alpha} < \qty{0.5}{\angstrom}$ and
	$W_{\mathrm{\raisebox{0.5pt}{\footnotesize[\raisebox{-0.5pt}{\footnotesize{NII}}]}}} < \qty{0.5}{\angstrom}$
	for passive, actually lineless galaxies, but based on our selection, none fall into this class. \Cref{fig:WHAN_class}
	shows the resulting categories, while \Cref{fig:WHAN_SDSS_class} presents the more restrictive SDSS classes, which
	show a smaller AGN region than what WHAN includes.

	\begin{figure}

		\caption{Colored WHAN diagram based on SDSS labels.}

	\end{figure}

	\subsection{Category Mixing}

	In addition to the predefined SDSS label, we can check the mixing between BPT and WHAN classes. For this,
	\Cref{fig:BPT_WHAN_class} displays WHAN colors in the BPT diagram, and \Cref{fig:WHAN_BPT_class} the
	opposite. Here we can see how BPT conditions do not acccount for weak equivalent widths, making WHAN a more
	nuanced model in this regard. On the other hand, WHAN labels many objects AGN that sit far down in the
	BPT scale, indicating that information on the ionization potential of the source gets lost.

	\subsection{WISE Colors}

	For sources shrouded by dust, optical classifier such as BPT and WHAN can fail. Instead, infrared data from
	missions such as WISE with filters labeled W1 at \qty{3.4}{\micro\meter} and W2 at \qty{4.6}{\micro\meter} as well
	as W3 at \qty{12}{\micro\meter} center wavelengths can be used. In these scenarios, galaxies dominated by their stars
	appear in the more energetic bands corresponding to stellar atmospheres, while an AGN torus absorbs shorter wavelengths,
	leading to smaller frequencies characterizing their spectrum. In the typical WISE colors plot, we draw the differences
	of the respective magnitudes, equivalent to the logarithm of the flux ratio and therefore independent of true luminosity.
	In this diagram, we can define a region where most AGN fall, referred to as the wedge from here on \cite{mate2012}. Our
	data is visualized and classified according to this wedge in \Cref{fig:WISE_overlay} with schematic classes from
	\cite{wrig2010} taken as a backdrop. Notably, there are only few objects being labeled as AGN by this method. For the actual
	statistics and a more robust comparison of the classifiers, the following subsection calculates the pairwise binary
	cross correlation and gives the raw numbers for our matched dataset.

	\begin{figure*}[t]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=\textwidth, draft=false]{assets/plots/WISE_overlay.pdf}
		\caption{Shaded scatter plot of data in WISE color diagram. Included is the wedge discriminator \cite{mate2012}
				 as well as the background adapted from \cite{wrig2010} with shortened object type labels.}

	\subsection{Evaluating Diagnostics}

	Listed in \Cref{tab:classes} are the numbers for our processed dataset. To better compare the performance between the
	different classifiers, we compute the so called Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) for each pair. This metric measures how
	the matching quality compares to a pure chance baseline. A value of 1 means perfect agreement, while 0 implies no
	advantage over randomly assigning an answer, and negative values signify worse than random anticorrelation between
	methods. It also corrects for unbalanced population sizes, as is the case for our sample in which AGN are far fewer than
	other types of galaxies. \Cref{fig:MCC_strict,fig:MCC_relaxed} show our results for two cases of BPT classifier, one
	strict which restricts positives to AGN objects only, and one relaxed that also includes COMP labels.

		\centering

				 used, of which \num{9799} had matches in the WISE database with a \ang{;;3} tolerance.}
		\begin{tabular}{l S[table-format=4.0] r}
			\toprule
			Method & Total & Fraction \\
			\midrule
			SDSS                  &  585 & \qty{5.9}{\percent} \\
			BPT (strict)          &  920 & \qty{9.2}{\percent} \\
			BPT (relaxed)         & 2491 & \qty{24.9}{\percent} \\

	than with infrared based results. On the other hand, agreement between WISE labels is barely
	better than random, especially for the WHAN results. For the strict BPT case, it matches best
	with SDSS classes, while the relaxed version shows better agreement with WHAN labels.

	\section{Physical Properties}

	Other than classifying objects, we can also extract estimates of their physical properties from spectral data.
	The following subsections will outline this process for electron density and gas velocity, as well as derive the
	central Black Hole (BH) mass for our dataset. Graphics in this section use hexagonal bins instead of
	scatter plots to retain the distribution shape while making possible trends in the data more visible.

	\subsection{Gas Density}

	First, we use the ratio of the two $\mathrm{\raisebox{0.75pt}{[\raisebox{-0.75pt}{NII}]}}$ lines at	\qty{6716}{\angstrom}
	and \qty{6731}{\angstrom} in our data. Since these both originate from a closely spaced doublet, their relative occurence
	is independent of gas temperature. Instead, at low densities the ratio is fixed by their degeneracies as statistical
	weights, whereas at high densities, collisional deexcitation becomes relevant, which does not emit photons. Due to the
	lower wavelength state having a longer lifetime, it gets affected more strongly by this mechanism, dropping the ratio and
	allowing us to reconstruct the local electron density as a proxy for the gas \cite{sand2016, joh2021}. The resulting
	values are depicted in \Cref{fig:BPT_density} and show a soft but clearly recognizable trend towards higher densities
	in the AGN classified region. This makes sense if we imagine that the harsh radiation field as well as shocks originating
	from the central SMBH compress the surrounding Narrow Line Region (NLR) gas from which our lines are emitting.

SELECT TOP 10000
    g.sii_6717_flux, g.sii_6731_flux, g.nii_6584_flux, g.oi_6300_flux, g.oiii_5007_flux,
    g.h_alpha_flux, g.h_beta_flux,
    g.h_alpha_eqw, g.nii_6584_eqw, g.oiii_sigma,
    w.w1mpro, w.w2mpro, w.w3mpro,
    s.z, s.class, s.subclass, s.bestobjid
FROM SpecObj AS s
    JOIN galSpecLine AS g ON s.specobjid = g.specobjid
    JOIN PhotoTag AS p ON s.bestobjid = p.objid
    OUTER APPLY (   SELECT TOP 1 x.wise_cntr
                    FROM wise_xmatch AS x
                    WHERE p.objid = x.sdss_objid AND x.match_dist < 3 
                    ORDER BY x.match_dist ASC
                ) AS y
    LEFT JOIN wise_allsky AS w ON y.wise_cntr = w.cntr
WHERE s.class = 'GALAXY'
    AND s.subclass != 'BROADLINE'
    AND s.subclass != 'AGN BROADLINE'
    AND s.subclass != 'STARFORMING BROADLINE'
    AND s.subclass != 'STARBURST BROADLINE'
    AND s.snmedian_r > 5.0
    AND s.z < 0.35
    AND g.sii_6717_eqw < 0.0
    AND g.sii_6731_eqw < 0.0
    AND g.nii_6584_eqw < 0.0
    AND g.oi_6300_eqw < 0.0
